Cracking your first technical interview

It is a conversation, not an exam. Here is how to prepare for it, think through it and talk through it.

01Mindset

Fundamentals used to be enough. Today you also need hands-on proof

Why the bar went up

  • More candidates per opening: more graduates, bootcamps, self-taught developers
  • The AI shift: routine code is cheap now, so judgement and problem solving matter more
  • Remote hiring means you compete with a much wider pool
  • Fewer junior openings after the industry's hiring slowdown
  • Online assessments filter hundreds of applicants before a human sees you

So what?

Knowing concepts gets you in the door. Showing us you have built things gets you the job.

Your projects are your experience

Academic, personal, hackathon, open source: put them on your CV and know them inside out.

Not to scare them: the good news is the "hands-on proof" is something they already have from coursework, they just need to present it.

01Mindset

You are choosing us as much as we are choosing you

We ask
  • Can this person do the work?
  • Will they learn and grow fast?
  • Will they work well in our team?
You ask
  • Do I like the domain you work in?
  • Do I want to learn your tech stack?
  • Does your culture suit how I work?

Not selected does not mean not good enough

When we do not select you, it often means not the best fit for that role, at that time. You will not fit everywhere, and you do not need to. Do not take it personally: learn from it and move to the next one.

Share a personal story of a rejection that turned out well, if you have one.

01Mindset

Spend 15 minutes researching the company, even for a career fair walk-in

What to find out

  • What we build: products, clients, domain (fintech, health, telco...)
  • Tech stack: job post, engineering blog, GitHub, engineers' LinkedIn
  • Culture and size: startup or enterprise, how teams work
  • Recent news: new product, funding, expansion

Use it: pick project examples that match our stack and domain. We notice when you did.

Ask us good questions

  • "What does a new graduate work on in the first three months?"
  • "How do code reviews and mentoring work in the team?"
  • "I saw you moved to microservices last year. How did that go?"
  • "What does success look like after six months?"

Questions show us genuine interest, and help you decide.

"Do you have any questions for us?" is not a formality for us. "No" is a missed chance.

02Projects & CV

Projects are your experience: list them, and be ready to explain every line

On the CV, for each project

  • The problem it solves, in one line
  • Your role and what you personally built
  • The stack you used
  • A result: users, speed, grade, award, a number if you have one

Example line

Campus Bus Tracker · group of 4 · Flutter, Node.js, PostgreSQL

Built the live-location API and map screen; cut location update delay from 10 s to 2 s using WebSockets. Used by 300+ students.

Every word here is a question you may be asked. If you cannot talk about it, remove it.

Academic projects count. Final-year project, group assignments, personal side projects, all of it.

02Projects & CV

We do not care how big the project was. We care what you did in it

Interviewer
Tell me about your final-year project.
Weak
It is a streaming platform like Netflix. It has login, recommendations, payments, an admin panel, and we used React, Spring Boot and MySQL...
Interviewer
(thinking) Okay, but what did you do?
Strong
I owned the recommendation service. I designed the ratings table, wrote the API, and brought response time from 2 s to 300 ms by caching popular results. The team was four people; the others did payments, UI and admin.

Even a Netflix-sized group project tells us nothing until we hear your role, your decisions and your contribution.

Ask the room: "Which answer lets us ask a good follow-up?" The strong one, because it has concrete hooks: caching, 2 s to 300 ms.

02Projects & CV

Prepare each project in four layers, not just the collaboration story

1 · Problem

What problem does it solve, and for whom? Why does it matter?

2 · Solution

Overall architecture. Main components and how they talk. Why this design?

3 · Your part

What you built, decisions you made, how you worked with the team (Git, reviews, conflicts).

4 · Lessons

What broke, how you fixed it, what you would do differently now.

Test yourself: can you draw it on a whiteboard in 60 seconds?

Mobile app → API server → database, plus whatever else exists (queue, cache, third-party APIs). If you cannot sketch your own system, revisit it before the interview.

Common follow-ups: "Why that database?" "What would happen with 10x users?" "What was the hardest bug?" "If you started again, what would you change?"

02Projects & CV

Never invent the story. Practise how you tell it

Do not fabricate

Follow-up questions go deep fast. A made-up story falls apart by the third "why", and so does our trust in you.

Do practise the articulation

Not a memorised script: know the points, then say them out loud until it comes out clear, in any order we ask.

Follow-ups are not a lie detector

When we ask "Why did you use MongoDB?", we are not checking if you really did it.

We want to see how you handled the problem: what you considered, what you chose, what you learned.

For "tell me about a time..." questions, use STAR: Situation, Task, Action (the biggest part), Result.

Remind them again: this is not a viva. We are not trying to catch anyone out.

03Answering well

Give your answer first, then the facts that support it

Interviewer
Which database would you choose for an online shop's orders and payments?
Answer · the anchor"I would pick PostgreSQL."
Reason 1: the data is relational: users, orders, payments
Reason 2: payments need transactions
Reason 3: the team is familiar with SQL already

Why it works

The listener gets a hook first, so every fact has a place to attach to. Neither of you gets lost.

Remember who is listening

We are busy, often tired after many candidates. Like you in a hard lecture: once we get lost, we stop listening.

Works everywhere: stand-ups, emails, code reviews, life. This deck follows it too: every headline is the answer.

Contrast: story first ("So first we looked at MySQL, then someone said MongoDB, then...") leaves the listener guessing where it is going.

It is a skill, not a talent. You only get it by practising.

03Answering well

If you do not know, say so clearly, then add what you do know

Interviewer
Have you worked with React?
Weak
Yes, a little... I mean, I have seen some tutorials, it uses components and, um, hooks and things...
Strong
No, not React. But I have built two apps with Angular and one with Vue, so components and state management are familiar. I would expect to pick React up quickly.

Bluffing loses our interest faster than a gap in knowledge. We do not expect you to know everything.

If what we need is far beyond what you have, that is fine too. It is a conversation: maybe this role is not the fit.

Same for technical questions: "I am not sure about the exact algorithm, but I know it uses a heap, so my guess is O(n log n)." Partial, honest reasoning beats a confident wrong answer.

03Answering well

Keep answers simple, but not awkwardly simple

Interviewer
Can you explain the difference between a queue and a stack?
Too short
Yes. (silence... now we have to say "Go on, explain")
Too long
Five minutes on arrays, linked lists, memory, Java's Deque class, and a story about a lab assignment...
Just right
Yes. The main difference is the order items come out. A queue is first in, first out, like a line at a counter. A stack is last in, first out, like a pile of plates.

Then stop. If we want more, we will ask, and that is a good sign, not a failure.

Point out: "Can you explain...?" is a polite way of saying "Explain". Answer the intent, not the literal yes/no.

03Answering well

Two questions in one breath? Relax, and answer both parts

Interviewer
Can you explain the difference between a queue and a stack, and where have you used them in your projects?
You
Two parts, let me take them in order. First, the difference: a queue is first in, first out, a stack is last in, first out. Second, in my food delivery project I used a queue for incoming orders so they were processed in arrival order, and a stack for the undo feature in the cart.

Signpost

"Two parts", "first", "second" keeps both of you on track.

Ask if needed

"Could you repeat the second part?" is perfectly fine.

Breathe

We are not there to rag you. It is a friendly conversation to check fit.

It is fine to take a few seconds of silence before answering a question. Saying "Let me think for a moment" is a strength, not a weakness.

04Problem solving

In problem solving we grade how you think, not only whether you finish

An unfinished solution with clear reasoning often beats a correct one written in silence.

1
Clarify
Inputs, size, edge cases, expected output. Never assume.
2
Examples
Work a normal and an edge case by hand.
3
Brute force
Say the simple way and its cost. Do not code it yet.
4
Optimise
Find the repeated work. Which structure removes it?
5
Code
Clean names, small steps, narrate as you go.
6
Test
Trace your own code on an example before saying "done".
✓Problem solving ✓Communication ✓Coding ✓Testing ✓Trade-offs

The chips are roughly what a typical interview rubric scores. The final answer is only one part of it.

04Problem solving

Think out loud: narrate your intent, not your keystrokes

The silent candidate

Two minutes of quiet typing. We cannot help, cannot give credit, and start to worry.

The thinking-aloud candidate

Even when you are stuck, we can see your reasoning and nudge you with a hint. Hints are part of the process.

Phrases to borrow

  • "Let me make sure I understand the problem..."
  • "A simple way is X. It is O(n²) because..."
  • "The repeated work is here, so a hash map could remove it."
  • "I am choosing between A and B. I lean to A because..."
  • "I am stuck on this part. Let me try a smaller example."
  • "Let me trace this with the example before I say it works."

When you get a hint: acknowledge it ("Ah, so sorting first would help because...") and build on it. Using a hint well is a good signal.

04Worked example 1 · warm-up

Pair with a target sum: show the trade-off, memory for speed

Given a list of numbers and a target, return the positions of two numbers that add up to the target. [2, 7, 11, 15], target 9 → [0, 1]

ClarifyCan there be negatives or duplicates? Is there always exactly one answer? Can I use the same element twice?
Brute forceThe simple way checks every pair, two loops. That is O(n²) time, O(1) memory.
OptimiseFor each number I really ask: have I already seen target minus this? A hash map answers that in O(1).
Trade-offSo O(n) time, but O(n) extra memory. If memory were tight, I could sort and use two pointers: O(n log n), but I lose the original positions.
TestTrace: 2, need 7, not seen, store 2. Then 7, need 2, seen at 0. Return [0, 1].
def two_sum(nums, target):
    seen = {}             # value -> index
    for i, x in enumerate(nums):
        need = target - x
        if need in seen:
            return [seen[need], i]
        seen[x] = i
    return []          # no pair

Very well known problem, which is the point: even on a known problem, we want to hear the clarifying questions and the trade-off, not a recited answer.

04Worked example 2 · real-world shape

Meeting rooms: draw it, spot the pattern, explain the choice

Given meeting times, find the minimum number of rooms needed. [(9,10), (9:30,12), (11,13)] → 2

ClarifyIf one meeting ends at 10 and another starts at 10, can they share a room? Are times already sorted? How many meetings, roughly?
Picture itLet me draw them on a timeline. The answer is the most meetings happening at the same moment.
Brute forceCheck every minute of the day and count overlaps. Works, but depends on time precision, not on n.
OptimiseSort by start time. Keep the end times of busy rooms in a min-heap. If the earliest-ending room is free, reuse it, else open a new one.
CostSorting is O(n log n), each heap step O(log n). Memory O(n) in the worst case, when all meetings overlap.
def min_rooms(meetings):
    meetings.sort()     # by start
    ends = []           # min-heap of ends
    for start, end in meetings:
        if ends and ends[0] <= start:
            heappop(ends) # reuse room
        heappush(ends, end)
    return len(ends)

Point out: drawing the timeline is "thinking aloud" on the whiteboard. Visualising turned a vague problem into "maximum overlap", which is the intervals pattern.

04Worked example 3 · open-ended

Rate limiter: when the question is vague, your questions are the answer

Write allow(user): each user may make at most 5 requests per minute. Return true or false.

ClarifyPer calendar minute, or any rolling 60 seconds? One server or many? How many users?
SimpleA counter per user that resets each minute. Tiny memory, but 5 at 0:59 and 5 at 1:00 lets 10 through in two seconds.
BetterKeep each user's recent request times in a queue and drop the ones older than 60 s. Exact, but stores up to 5 times per user.
Trade-offSo it is accuracy against memory. With millions of users across many servers, I would move the counts to a shared store like Redis.
TestEdge cases: exactly 60 s apart, a brand new user, a burst of 6 at once.
log = defaultdict(deque)  # user -> times

def allow(user, now):
    q = log[user]
    while q and q[0] <= now - 60:
        q.popleft()       # older than 1 min
    if len(q) < 5:
        q.append(now)
        return True
    return False

This one bridges coding and system design. Even if a candidate never finishes the code, asking the window question and naming the accuracy vs memory trade-off shows us exactly what we wanted to see.

05DSA patterns

Learn to recognise patterns, do not memorise solutions

"Have I seen this before?" pairs, counts
Hash map
Sorted array, find a pair or remove duplicates
Two pointers
Best contiguous subarray or substring
Sliding window
Sorted data, or "smallest value that works"
Binary search
Matching brackets, undo, next greater element
Stack
Shortest path in a grid, level by level
BFS
Explore all paths, connected regions
DFS
Top k, next smallest, merge sorted lists
Heap
All combinations, permutations, subsets
Backtracking
Overlapping ranges, schedules
Sort + intervals
Count the ways, best choice with overlapping subproblems
Dynamic programming
Cycle or middle of a linked list
Fast & slow pointers

A memorised answer breaks when the question changes one detail. A recognised pattern adapts.

Our three worked examples used three of these: hash map, heap with intervals, queue as a sliding window.

05DSA patterns

Read the input size: it tells you how fast your solution must be

n ≤ 20O(2ⁿ)Try every option: backtracking
n ≤ 500O(n³)Triple loops are fine
n ≤ 5,000O(n²)Check every pair
n ≤ 10⁶O(n log n)Sort, heap, binary search
largerO(n) · O(log n)One pass, or math

Practise with understanding

  • Group problems by pattern, one pattern at a time
  • Solve, then explain it aloud as if to an interviewer
  • Write down the signal that pointed to the pattern
  • Re-solve a week later without looking
  • 60 to 80 well-understood problems beat 500 rushed ones

Rule of thumb: about 10⁸ simple operations per second. Also share the "stuck" checklist: smaller example, sort the input, try a hash map, work backwards, solve a simpler version.

06System design

System design has no single right answer: it is all trade-offs

The key habit: "It depends, and here is on what." You cannot have everything, something has to be sacrificed.

Low latency⇄Fresh data
Consistency⇄Availability
Performance⇄Cost
Simplicity⇄Scalability

Example: when latency is critical, accept some staleness

Serve from a cache and update it with events in the background (an event-driven approach). Users get fast answers, but may see data that is a few seconds old. Say that cost out loud.

Entry level: expect light, open-ended design questions. We do not expect a complete design. We want to see you reason about the trade-offs.

Ask the room for an everyday example: a "like" count can be a few seconds stale; a bank balance cannot.

06System design

Ask clarifying questions before designing anything. Never assume

Questions that change the design

  • Who uses it, and how many? 1,000 or 10 million?
  • Mostly reads or mostly writes?
  • What matters most: speed, accuracy, or cost?
  • Can any data be stale? For how long?
  • What happens if it goes down for a minute?

Assuming

Jumps straight to microservices and Kafka for a system with 200 users.

Asking

Good questions tell us you have real skill, before you draw a single box.

Same rule as coding: clarify first. The answers turn an impossible question into a specific one.

06System design · walkthrough

A URL shortener in five moves, trade-offs named out loud

  1. Requirements: shorten a link, redirect fast, count clicks
  2. Scale: ~100 reads per write, so optimise reads
  3. API & data: POST /links, GET /{code} · table code → url
  4. Boxes: load balancer, app servers, cache, database
  5. Trade-offs: click counts go through a queue, so stats lag a little but redirects stay fast

Vocabulary to introduce lightly: load balancer, cache, index, queue, SQL vs NoSQL. The point is not the boxes, it is saying why each box is there and what it costs.

07Practice

Clear communication is a skill: you only get it by practising out loud

Alone

Explain a project or a solution to your phone camera. Watch it back. Count the "ums" and the silent gaps.

With friends

Explain a problem to a friend who stops you every time they get lost.

Mock interviews

Groups of three: interviewer, candidate, observer. 30 minutes, then rotate.

Observer's checklist

1Answer first? 2Asked clarifying questions? 3Thought out loud? 4Named a trade-off? 5Honest about gaps? 6Tested the solution?

Suggest they start a weekly mock group this week. Nothing prepares you like being on the interviewer side for once.

07Practice

Use AI as a coach that gives feedback, not a crutch that answers for you

Good uses

  • Polish answers: write your own answer, ask for feedback on clarity and structure
  • Mock interviewer: give it your CV and let it ask follow-up questions
  • Review your code after you solve it: edge cases, complexity, naming
  • Explain back: explain a pattern, ask what you got wrong
  • Research: summarise a company's products and stack
"Here is my answer to 'Tell me about your project'. Do not rewrite it. Tell me where an interviewer would get lost, and whether my answer comes first."

Avoid

  • Memorising AI-written scripts
  • Letting it invent experience you do not have
  • Practising only with autocomplete on

In the interview it is just you and your reasoning, so practise without assistance too. Also: we can usually tell a rehearsed AI script from a real story.
